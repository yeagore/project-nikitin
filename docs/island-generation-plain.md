# Island generation, explained plainly

This is the whole of `docs/island-generation.md`, its appendix and the dev-scenes
manual retold for someone who does not read code. (For Claude: this file is a
retelling, not a source; the spec is. Keep it true when the spec changes, and do
not read it for orientation.) Nothing is left out on
purpose; where the spec gives a number, the number is here too, with what it
means. Words the project uses in its own way (Domain, slab, Gate, aether) are
explained the first time they appear and again in the glossary at the end.

---

## 1. What is being made

The game's world is a tree of floating islands, each called a **Domain**,
hanging in a hazardous void called the **aether** and joined by magical
**Links**, with a built **Gate** at each end of a Link. A Domain is what the
player explores and settles; one Domain at a time is generated by the code this
document describes.

### The island as a grid of columns

Picture a square chessboard, 64, 96 or 128 squares on a side. Each square is a
**cell**, one metre across in the game's units; in the fiction a cell is about
the size of an orchard. Standing on every land cell is a **column** of solid
ground, made of **slabs**: flat blocks one cell wide and a quarter of a cell
tall. Four slabs stack to the height of one cell. All heights in the generator
are counted in slabs, as whole numbers.

A column is stored as a short list of solid **runs** from bottom to top. Almost
every column is one run, from the island's underside up to the ground you walk
on. A few columns have two runs with air between them: that is an overhang, or
one cell of a natural arch. Caves that branch are not supported; the model is
"ground, and maybe a roof over it".

Three ways of reading a column are used everywhere:

- the **surface**: the top of the lowest run, the ground you stand on;
- the **keel**: the bottom of the lowest run, the underside of the island;
- the **effective level**: the water surface if the cell is under water, the
  ground otherwise. Anything that describes what a place looks like (climate,
  cliffs, banks) reads this one, because a river bank is a bank, not a cliff
  over the river's bed.

### The one rule that shapes everything

**A step of one slab is free. A step of two or more slabs is a cliff.** People
walk up and down single slabs for nothing; anything taller needs something
built, a stair or a hoist. The whole surface is built so that walking costs
nothing by default, and every cliff on the island is there because some rule
put it there on purpose. Walking includes diagonal moves, except where both
cells beside the diagonal are cliffs, since then there is no corner to cut.
Built things (bridges, stairs, quays) stay on the straight axes.

A step of exactly two slabs is the worst kind: too tall to walk, too short to
read as a cliff. The generator hunts these down and removes them.

### The invisible box

Every Domain sits in a cube as tall as it is wide: 128 cells across, 128 slabs
tall, for the biggest size. Nothing the generator makes may stick out of it,
Gates included. Because the box is fixed, a mountain on a small island is
proportionally lower than one on a big island rather than a scale model of the
same mountain: the tallest mountain allowed is 40 slabs at 128 cells, and
scales down with the footprint; the underside's deepest keel is 34 slabs.

### Same seed, same island

Generation is a pure function of two inputs: a **seed** (a number) and the
**settings**. The same pair always produces the identical island, on either of
the two development machines, bit for bit. Every random choice inside is made
from the seed plus a fixed "salt" for that particular choice, so one stage's
dice never disturb another's. Every scan of the grid runs in one fixed order,
so ties always break the same way.

If an island comes out unplayable (no way in, no way out, no road to an exit,
nowhere to build, or split into pieces that cannot be joined), the generator
quietly tries again with a new seed derived from the first, up to four times,
and if none passes it keeps the one that failed the fewest tests and says which
tests it failed.

## 2. The settings

There are ten dials that run from 0 to 1, all left on **Auto** by default. Auto
means the seed rolls that dial for each island, evenly across its whole range,
so consecutive seeds differ in weather and water as well as in shape. The island
remembers the values it was actually built with, and the lab shows them.

| dial | what it does |
|---|---|
| Landform mix | how much of the island is high ground rather than plain: 0 mostly plains, 1 as much high ground as the island's character allows |
| Relief | how exaggerated every landform's ups and downs are |
| Hilliness | what hills do: 0 gentle swells, 1 steep mounds (still one slab at a time); also how jagged the surface noise is |
| Rivers | how wet the island is: the amount of upstream land a channel needs before it counts as a river |
| Lakes | how readily standing water collects: 0 none, 1 a lake in every flat patch that could hold one |
| Valleys | how far the ground falls toward a river: 0 a bare cut, 1 five cells of valley either side |
| Moisture | the background dampness of the ground before any water adds to it: about 0.15 is dry country, 0.45 balanced, 0.75 wet |
| Warmth | the warmth of open flat lowland: under about 0.3 is cold country, 0.5 temperate, over about 0.7 hot, the last twentieth desert. Even 0 keeps the lowland above the snow |
| Wind | how hard the island's one wind blows: 0 still air, where shelter changes nothing; 0.5 the normal effects; 1 twice them |
| Overhang density | how often a tall cliff is undercut and a short gap arched over |

Other settings are fixed numbers rather than dials:

- **Size**: 64, 96 or 128 cells, or Auto to roll one of the three.
- **Coverage** (0.62): how much of each land blob's disc survives as land.
- **Irregularity** (0.55): how far the outline departs from a circle.
- **Arrangement**: the layout of the land (thirty of them, or Auto). **New
  arrangements** and **new landforms**: switches that keep the newer layouts and
  the sculpted characters out of Auto's dice without removing them; naming one
  by hand still builds it.
- **Character**: which landforms the island is built from (Plains, Tablelands,
  Downs, Highlands, Badlands, Karst, Massif, Dunes, or Auto).
- **Region scale** (16 cells): the typical width of one patch of the island's
  patchwork; the smallest patch allowed follows from it (55 cells at 16).
- **Cliff height** (4 slabs) and **plateau levels** (2): the plateau ladder,
  explained below. **Mountain height** (40 slabs, capped by the box), **mesa
  height** and **basin depth** (5 slabs each).
- **Goo**: whether an island may roll goo puddles at all.
- **Crossings**: Easy, Medium or Hard, meaning a bridge may span 1, 3 or 6
  cells. This also decides how far apart an archipelago's pieces may sit and
  what counts as reachable once you build.
- **Entry gate** and **entry edge**: inputs, not choices; they must match the
  Domain you arrived from. **Exit gates** (1 to 3, or 0 to let the seed decide)
  and **exit gate kind**.
- **Edge thickness** (3 slabs), **keel depth** (34), **keel roughness** (0.45):
  the underside. **Overhang depth** (2 cells) and **arch span** (4 cells).

Some things are deliberately not dials: the free step (one slab, everywhere),
the smallest patch size, the shape of the underside, and the settlement
thresholds. A dial that varied any of them would change what a cliff means.

## 3. The pipeline, stage by stage

The island is built in a fixed order, each stage reading what the one before
left. Nothing may be reordered.

### Stage 1: the footprint (the outline of the land)

**Blobs.** The land begins as a set of overlapping blobs, one or more per
landmass, each an ellipse whose radius wanders on a noise pattern so that no
coastline is a circle. The **arrangement** says how the blobs are laid out.
Where two blobs meet, the seam is either left alone (the blobs fuse into one
mass) or carved into a strait (a gap of aether that can pinch to a single cell
but never closes). That one choice is the whole difference between a Ring and a
Broken Ring. Blobs can be grouped, and only seams between groups are cut. Two
"clearings" override blobs outright: a **lagoon** (a disc of aether, at a ring's
centre or off-centre for a block's hole) and a **waist** (two bays that pinch a
neck to a set width).

**The ragged coast.** Each cell belongs to the nearest blob, and each blob keeps
only its best share of its disc: a noise pattern ranks the blob's cells and the
lowest 38% are dropped. The pattern is drawn at the island's scale, not the
cell's, so big and small islands get the same kind of coast. Ground inside two
blobs at once is never dropped: it is interior by construction.

**The thirty arrangements.** Single (one landmass); Satellites (one landmass
with two to four islets round it); Twins, Triplets, Archipelago (two, three, or
five to eight comparable masses); Shards (one mass cracked into four to six by
narrow straits); Ring and Broken Ring, Arc and Broken Arc (a ring or crescent
round a lagoon or bay, fused or parted); Caldera (a ring round an inner island,
the moat between them the only way in); Atoll (beads on a string, a step of
water between each); Cross, T, L, Star and their broken forms (a hub with thick
arms pointing at the edges, so an arm points at a Gate; the broken forms part
the arms); Fractal (a chain that turns as it goes); Rosette (narrow petals fused
at a small heart); Thousand Isles (a jittered quilt of 4×4 to 6×6 blobs, every
seam a strait); Square and Rhomb (blocky grids of blobs, sometimes with a hole);
N (the letter); Quarters and Halves (one mass sliced twice or once); Harmony
(the yin-yang); Isthmus (two heads joined by a neck); Reef (a main island behind
a long thin barrier). Auto draws from a weighted pool that leans toward a single
landmass (Single has weight 34 of about 133).

**Tidying.** A Single or Satellites island gets one or two **bites** taken out
along natural borders (whole patches of a draft partition deleted). Cells that
touch only at a corner within one landmass are filled in, since a corner is not
a join you can walk (but two islands touching at a corner are never welded).
Pieces under 30 cells are dropped as too small to matter.

**Sea stacks.** Of those dropped specks, two or three (the largest, still wholly
in the aether and not touching land on a straight side) are kept as **sea
stacks**: pillars off the coast, listed for the content layer but not land,
so nothing walks, builds or flies through them. A stack under a Gate's flight
path is removed. Most seeds leave no speck to keep, so stacks are rare (about
one island in seven).

**The linker.** Every piece is then nudged toward its neighbours until each
faces another across no more than a bridge's span (3 cells at the Medium
setting), on a straight line. The linker drags the closest stray piece the whole
way at once, slides pieces that have no straight line of sight until they do,
and after forty rounds deletes whatever still cannot be linked, so that the
"everything can be joined by building" promise always holds. The crossing sites
it finds are recorded as the future bridges.

**The fit pass.** The landmass's bounding rectangle must cover 55 to 85 percent
of the grid, measured after the bites, the filter and the linker. Outside that
band the layout is scaled about the centre and redrawn, up to three times. A
scaled blob is kept inside the grid by its own reach on each axis, so two
halves pushed apart stay apart.

### Stage 2: regions and landforms

**The patchwork.** The land is cut into patches, like counties with wobbly
borders: a jittered grid of seed points, each cell going to its nearest seed
through a warping noise, split into connected pieces and merged until no patch
is under the minimum size.

**The relief envelope.** A gentle map in the range 0 to 1 says where this
island's high ground tends to be: a tilt, a broad flat, a spine, or a pair of
masses, chosen per character. It biases which patches go high and where
mountains cluster; it never shapes the ground directly.

**The plateau ladder.** Every patch stands on a **rung**: a whole number of
cliff heights (4 slabs each) above the coastal level. The number of rungs is the
plateau-levels setting (2 by default). Two neighbouring patches on the same rung
are joined ground; the smoothing reaches across their border and holds it to
the free step. Two patches on different rungs meet at a cliff of exactly one
cliff height. So which borders are cliffs is decided up front, and a cliff is a
decision, never an accident of the noise. At one rung the only cliffs come from
mesas, basins and mountains; at five the island is a flight of terraces.

**The ten landforms.** Each patch is given one:

| landform | what it is |
|---|---|
| Plain | flat, give or take a slab: buildable, crossable, dull on purpose |
| Hills | rolling ground in one-slab steps, up to about 15 slabs of rise; walkable everywhere |
| Dunes | long parallel ridges running one way (along the island's wind): level along, washboard across |
| Mountain | an S-curve rising from foothills to a rugged summit; the only landform with no slope limit, so its flanks are cliffs |
| Mesa | a flat top standing clear above every neighbour, ringed by cliff |
| Basin | a mesa inverted: a flat floor sunk below every neighbour, ringed by an inward-facing cliff |
| Badlands | a plain with gullies 5 slabs deep cut through it: flat fingers and a maze of ravines |
| Karst | a flat floor you walk with towers 13 slabs tall you cannot climb |
| Massif | concentric terraces climbing to a flat summit, each riser 4 slabs and wanting a stair |
| Sinkholes | open ground pocked with round pits 6 slabs deep |

**The character is a quota, not a dice roll.** Plains has only plains;
Tablelands plains, mesas and basins; Downs plains and hills; Highlands plains,
hills and mountains; Badlands, Karst, Massif and Dunes add their namesake to
plains and hills. Every landform a character names gets at least one patch, and
the shares are counts handed out by rank on the envelope (mountains to the high
ground, basins to the low, hills between) with a per-patch jitter so the island
is not banded like a contour map. The landform-mix dial tilts the counts from
low ground to high.

**Adjacency rules.** A mesa may touch only plains (beside a mountain the mesa
gives way; any other neighbour is flattened, which makes the apron a mesa needs
to read as one). Adjacent mountains merge into one massif, because a mountain
penned in one patch has no room for a foot. A patch that holds a bridgehead
becomes a plain, since a mesa or mountain would ignore the rung agreement the
two banks need. The quota is restored last, after everything that flattens a
patch has had its say.

**The island's wind.** One direction for the whole island, snapped to a compass
point, rolled for every island whether or not it has dunes. Dune fields lie
across it, and the climate reads it for shelter.

### Stage 3: the surface

Each patch gets its own relief on its rung, under its own slope limit: rung plus
a little noise for a plain, rung plus a bigger swell for hills, a wave along the
wind for dunes, an S-curve for a mountain whose foot is read from the real
ground at its border and blurred so it joins flush. Then the surface is
**settled** by two passes that only ever lower ground:

- **Limit slope**: any cell standing higher than its patch allows above a
  neighbour is lowered, repeatedly, until the whole patch obeys its limit. It
  reaches across borders between patches on the same rung, which is what closes
  the cliffs the rules forbid. Cells that are exempt (a sculpted cut, a canyon
  floor, a lake bed) are neither lowered nor used as a bound, since a lake bed
  taken as a bound would drag its whole rung down into it.
- **Resolve ambiguous steps**: every two-slab step is removed.

Between settles, the deliberate exceptions are cut in. The **sculpted
landforms** are carved into the settled surface and then exempted from the
limiter; that is how they carry cliffs inside a patch, which relief under a
slope limit cannot express. Two rules keep them honest: nothing is sculpted in
the outermost ring of its own patch, so every border stays bound; and every cut
is a fixed depth, because a tapering gully has a two-slab step somewhere by
construction. A lone pit or pillar is undone, since one cell is an orchard.

One island in five gets a **canyon**: a cliff cut across a border where the rules
would otherwise forbid one. And **passes** do the opposite: where a cliff border
separates two plateaus, a saddle is cut so that one plateau sags to meet the
next, and the border gets exactly one place you can walk across.

### Stage 4: standing water

**Lakes** sink into the interior of flat patches (plains, mesas, basins) after
the smoothing they must not undo and before the underside is measured. A lake
needs no wall of high ground: the patch's own untouched rim is the containment.
At least two cells of rim stay dry all round, and the shoreline wanders a few
cells further on a noise pattern, so a lake is the patch's shape read through
that pattern rather than a scale copy of a polygon. The step from rim to water
is one slab, so shores are walkable; the bed drops three or four, clear of the
ambiguous two. One lake per patch, and a patch beside one that holds water stays
dry, because a row of pools at slightly different levels reads as flooding. The
lakes dial scales how many; a large flat interior lifts the chance; a mesa
rarely gets one, and then only a small tarn. A patch cut by a canyon or a pass
holds no water, since it would fill to the bottom of the cut and pour out.

**Shapes.** A pool large enough to have an inside rolls a shape: still one body
more often than not, else a scatter of separate pools ("thousand lakes"), a ring
round a dry islet of its own floor, a crescent, a ragged cross, or a tarn
cropped small. Every shape is a subset of the pool the rim already approved.

**Goo.** Each column records what fluid stands in it. Water is the default and
the only fluid that behaves: rivers, ferries and fords are water's alone. About
three islands in ten roll one to three **goo** puddles instead, placed like small
tarns in dry flat patches. Goo makes no rivers, and no water may stand within a
king's move of it, not even diagonally; the placement guarantees this, the river
stage preserves it, and the audit counts it. What goo is for is the biome
layer's decision; today it is inert. **Geysers** are an empty hook: the data
has a list and the lab can draw jets, but nothing fills the list, because where
a jet belongs is a fact about the biome.

### Stage 5: beaches and settling

Where the ground meets the rim gently (a plain, hills or dunes, level with its
neighbours), the outermost cell steps down one slab in stretches chosen by a
slow noise pattern, so a **beach** is a strand along part of a coast, about a
third of it, not a shelf ringing the island. It is a shape and an anchor for the
content layer, not a material: it wears whatever ground the climate gives it.

Then the two ends of every future bridge are brought to one level, because a
bridge is a run of slabs at one height and does not climb; the levelling only
lowers, refuses to gouge a stair's worth off a coast, and will not touch ground
beside a lake. The three lowering passes (bridgeheads, slope limit, two-slab
removal) then take turns until nothing moves, since each can expose work for
the next. All three only lower, so the loop ends.

### Stage 6: rivers

There is no sea. Every river ends at the rim and pours off into the aether,
with one deliberate exception below.

**Routing.** Terrain under a slope limit is mostly flat, so "flow downhill"
stalls everywhere. Instead the island is flooded inward from its rim: every cell
gets a downstream neighbour by construction, the flood passes straight through
lakes, and a depression fills and spills at its lowest rim without a special
case. Ties are broken on a smooth noise pattern, which is what makes rivers bend
(a first-come tie-break makes a fan of dead-straight rays).

**Sources** are named rather than found: one outflow per lake (the shore cell
whose downstream ground is lowest) and summits in order of height, spaced apart
so a single massif does not spend the whole budget, two to six of them by the
rivers dial. Each source is traced down to the rim, adding a river's worth of
flow along the way, so two courses that meet make one wider than either. A
channel counts as a **river** where its upstream land passes a bar the dial
sets (about 5.5% of the land, eased by the dial), and as **navigable** where it
passes twice that (about 11%): two cells wide, three slabs deep, too wide to
wade, the second cell added on the lower side. On an Easy-crossings island
nothing is navigable, since a two-cell river a bridge cannot span would cut the
country in half.

**Eyots.** About one navigable reach in five splits round a strip of island
lying along the course, at least two cells of island with water at both ends.
The eyot stands a slab clear of the water.

**Beds and banks.** A stream's bed is cut two slabs below the ground and filled
to one below, so the banks stand proud and the water reads as a channel; a
navigable river is cut three deep. Every course is then forced downhill (the
routing promised a downstream neighbour, not a lower one), the two cells of a
navigable pair are held to one level, and the banks are brought down to the
free step: a bank exactly two slabs above the water comes down one, the
correction walking outward; a bank three or more above is a gorge wall, which
the grammar allows. Ground whose height is the point of it (a mesa rim, a
tower, a levelled bridgehead) is never cut.

**Valleys.** The ground either side of a course sinks toward it in whole bands,
so the only step it makes is the one slab between bands, and the channel sinks
one band deeper than its own bank (lowering only the ground beside a river makes
a moat, not a valley). The valleys dial works per watercourse: each drainage
draws a rank, and the dial slides a window across the ranks, tilted so that at
mid-slider the valleys go to courses that came down through uneven country
while a river crossing a plain keeps its bare cut. Because these passes only
lower, a pass afterwards lifts any shore that ended up under the water beside it.

**A stair of pools.** A navigable river is walked from the rim upstream and held
level until the ground has risen a fall's worth (3 slabs); then it steps. The
descend and pair-levelling corrections are run against each other until both
hold at once.

**Falls.** A cell that is a fall at all (aether beside it, or a fall's depth of
drop along its course) throws a sheet off every aether edge beside it and toward
every neighbouring water a fall's depth below; a lake pours too where its
outflow leaves well under its surface. Never onto dry ground, so nothing new
gets wet. Rim falls are drawn spilling 16 slabs past the underside.

**A lake that swallows a river.** On about three islands in ten that have a
river-fed lake, one lake (a basin's for preference, else the one with the
strongest inflow) is made a sink after the first accounting: its cells lose
their downstream neighbour, the flows are summed again, nothing is traced past
it and it has no outflow. This breaks "every course reaches the aether" on
purpose, and the island remembers which lake did it.

**Deltas.** A navigable river that meets the rim over a gentle coast parts into
two or three mouths. From the river's axis four cells upstream of the mouth, an
arm leaves each side of the pair (a step sideways, two forward, and again, on
the straight axes) until it reaches the rim, as a stream of its own. An arm that
would climb, drop more than a slab, run beside standing water, or take a
bridgehead, an eyot or a cell the river already holds is not cut; one that finds
no rim within nine cells, or finds it before two cells forward (a river running
along the coast), is given up. The dry ground between the mouths is the fan, and
the surface stage gives it the wet ground of its climate.

**Springs** are where a stream begins on dry ground: a channel cell nothing
drains into, not beside a lake and not a delta's arm.

**Fords.** A stream is crossed on foot only at a ford: one at the head of each
course and one every 11 cells along it on flat ground, stretching to every 33
through broken ground (the same measure of local relief the climate uses), where
both banks are dry and within a slab of the water. Everywhere else a stream is
an obstacle. A short course still gets one.

### Stage 7: the keel, and packing

The underside is an absolute level, not a thickness subtracted from the surface
(that would mirror the relief downward): thin at the coast (3 slabs), deepening
inland to 34, sampled through a warp so it is not a surface of revolution, and
every column at least 3 slabs thick. Then the columns are written out with their
landform, water, fluid, canyon and pass flags; the crossings are recorded as
built (a deck halfway between the banks, so each end is a one-slab step); rim
falls are dropped past the underside now that it is known; and the fords are
marked.

### Stage 8: reading the terrain back

Nothing here changes the ground; it measures whether the island is playable.

**Walk areas**: what connects on foot by free steps, king's moves included, with
a diagonal blocked only when both cells beside it are cliffs. Water is not
ground; a stream is crossed at a ford. The areas are listed largest first. A
walk area of twenty cells or more is a **district**, and a district is
**somewhere to build**: walk-connected ground that needs no works. (There used to
be a separate idea, shelves, patches of level ground with a measured width. It
never read as a place and was removed; everything that used it now reads
districts.)

**Reach areas**: what connects once the player can build, with three kinds of
works. A **stair or hoist** climbs a face of up to 8 slabs, enough for a mesa or
basin rim, not a mountain flank; it stands on two cells that may not be a quay,
a bridgehead or a Gate's ground. A **bridge** joins land to land on a straight
line across at most the crossings span of aether, 3 cells of water, or a chasm
(ground 5 slabs or more below the deck, which is how one cliff top is bridged to
another); a deck is level and its banks within a slab of it. A **ferry** runs
between two quays on one body of water, however far apart. Diagonals are
walked, never built across. The largest reach area is the **heartland**.

**Water bodies** are connected sheets of standing fluid, and a waterfall cuts a
body in two, since nothing sails up one. **Ferry berths** are found by a domino
rule (a walkable quay within two slabs of sailable water, with somewhere to
unload behind it) and then pruned: the reach flood is run once without ferries,
and a body keeps its berths only if they land in two or more different pieces of
that answer, so the berths that survive exist because the water is genuinely in
the way. In practice every body can be bridged and no berth survives, so the
ferry machinery is idle today.

**The mainland is where you land.** Once the Gates are placed, the mainland and
heartland are re-pointed to the ground under the Entry's landing, because
"largest area" can name a mainland across a strait from the only way in.

### Stage 9: Gates

A Gate is one block: a cell wide, a cell deep, four slabs tall. Every Domain gets
one **Entry** and one to three **Exits**, at most one per edge and on that edge,
because Domains sit on a plane at their position in the world tree and a Gate
facing east that is not the easternmost thing on the map points back over the
Domain it leaves.

**Four hanging Gates first, then take away.** Every Domain is first given a
hanging Gate on each of its four edges, the maximum, chosen as a set by a small
search over the best candidates per edge, because each Gate has to out-reach
every other on both axes and a greedy pass paints itself into a corner. Then the
settings reduce that: an Exit the Domain does not need is deleted, a Gate asked
to be a land Gate is moved down onto its own landing strip, and the Entry is
whichever edge the world tree names.

- A **hanging** Gate floats five cells off the rim and is flown through. It
  needs clear air for the last three cells of the approach and a 1 × 3 landing
  strip running inland from the coast under it.
- A **land** Gate is the same site with the portal on the strip; you walk
  through it onto the ground a vessel would have landed on.

**The strip is built, not found**: its three cells are levelled to the height of
the innermost one, so the walk off the strip is exactly what the terrain made it
and only the cells toward the rim move. Placement is therefore the one pass that
both reads the analysis and moves slabs, so the analysis runs again after it.
Each Gate has an **apron**, the largest district within four cells of the strip's
head, capped at 400 cells so it stays a tie-break rather than the whole score;
roads run apron to apron. Nothing may hang outside the invisible box.

**A way in and out are guaranteed.** Where a coast will not take four Gates under
the full rules, the rules give a rung at a time: the edge band widens, then the
corner inset goes, then the separation between Gates falls (never below a sixth
of the footprint), and finally the dominance order gives. The band widens but
never disappears, since a Gate far from its edge leaves most of the Domain
behind the player as they arrive. Only an edge with no candidate at all comes
back empty.

The Entry's edge and kind are inputs from the sending Domain (a Link joins two
Gates of the same kind, and travelling east arrives on the west edge); they are
applied directly and checked at the end.

### Stage 10: roads

From the Entry's apron to each Exit's, the road that needs the least building:
walking is free, and every stair, bridge or ferry costs one. Cost is compared
works first, then length, so among equally cheap roads the shortest wins; a
ford costs eight cells of length, so a road crosses a stream rather than walking
down it. About a third of roads can simply be walked. Five elevators inside
fifteen cells is a **flight**, and a road with one is telling you it is going
the wrong way; the Domain is marked rough, which is hard country, not a fault.

### Stage 11: the habitat vector (climate)

Climate is read off the finished terrain, with no weather simulation, as six
numbers per column from 0 to 255, kept as separate axes so the later biome layer
can combine them rather than unpick one score. Two things are rolled per island
and read here: the wind (with the dunes) and a **sun**, a compass point the sun
stands at.

**Moisture** (parched to waterside). The background from the moisture dial,
wobbled up or down by up to 25 in soft patches. Then the wind: the lee of the
wind is a **rain shadow**, sheltered ground losing up to 30 (rain falls on the
windward side), while ground that is both sheltered and broken (a gorge floor
under its walls) gains up to 70 and goes mossy where the flat plateau above it
stays dry; both scale with the wind dial. Rock landforms and three cells round
them carry patches of drought (minus 60). Then what fresh water adds, goo adding
nothing: 200 at the bank, decaying over five cells of **walk cost** and gone by
sixteen, where walk cost counts a cell per cell along or downhill and two more
per slab climbed (except the free step up onto the bank), so a river waters the
plain it crosses and not the mountain it passes. The strip is wobbled by noise
so it is not a contour map of the water network.

**Warmth** (frozen to desert). The lowland's warmth from the dial, 60 at 0 and
240 at 1. A **lapse** applies to mountains alone, measured from each mountain's
own foot: nothing for the first 40% of the tallest mountain the footprint
allows, then the full loss over the next 60%, so a full-height mountain is snow
at its top in any climate, a half-height one is merely cold, and no terrace,
mesa or massif is ever cold. Then small touches, kept small so an island's mean
warmth reads at its dial: a slope turned to the **sun** up to 8 warmer and one
turned away 8 colder (flat ground untouched); **frost hollows**, every basin
floor and every sinkhole pit 8 colder; the lee up to 10 milder by the wind
dial; the rim 6 colder, fading over four cells inland; **hot water** (below);
and wet ground pulled 30% of the way toward the temperate middle, since water
tempers heat and cold alike.

**Hot springs and pools.** On a Domain whose warmth dial is under 0.35, each
spring has a chance (40% at a dial of 0, fading to nothing at 0.35) and each
small pool of standing water with no watercourse through it (35%) of running
hot. A hot source adds 90 warmth at itself, decaying over four cells of walk
cost, but never lifts a cell past the temperate band: a meadow in the tundra,
never a floodplain.

**Ruggedness** (flat to broken): how much the effective surface rises and falls
within two cells, 32 per slab, saturating at eight slabs, with water read as its
bank so a stream through a plain is flat country and a gorge is still its walls.

**Exposure** (sheltered to windswept): walking up to ten cells upwind, the
tallest rise found is cover, eight slabs being full shelter; a walk that leaves
the island gets the wind off the aether. This is geometry; the wind dial moves
what exposure does, not exposure itself.

**Rim distance**: cells of land between the column and the aether. The setting's
own axis: essence coral grows on rims, and the deep interior is sheltered
country.

**Water distance**: the walk cost to fresh water the moisture strip was read
from, kept whole to 255 for the settlement and biome layers, whose first
question about a site is whether water is near.

Beside these, and not part of the climate, every column has a **magickal
density**: for now pure noise in soft waves about forty cells long, read by
nothing, so that the lab, the audit and the collages carry the layer from the
start.

### Stage 12: surfaces and anchors

**Anchors** are the lists the content layer will read instead of placing things
at coordinates: coast cells; cliff brinks (dry cells three or more slabs above a
neighbour's effective surface, so a gorge rim counts and a river bank does not)
and cliff feet; banks (the walkable wet margin, at most a slab over the water);
river beds and lake beds; summits (the highest dry cells of genuinely high
country, at least half the mountain cap above the lowest ground, spaced apart,
so a flat island honestly has none); overhang lips; beaches, fords, Gate
landings, ferry quays; springs, falls, hot water; sea stacks; the lakes that
swallowed a river and the deltas. The lists overlap freely; only the lab's
flattened view has to pick one colour per cell.

**Materials.** Each column gets a provisional material so the island reads as a
place before the biome layer exists. In order of precedence:

1. A river or lake bed is **silt**, and nothing else is. A goo pool's bed and
   shore are **stone**.
2. Warmth under 35 is **snow**: the extreme cold, and a mountain's top.
3. Rock is where rock is. A tall face (six slabs) bares **stone** at its brink
   and drops **scree** at its foot whatever the landform; a rock landform
   (mountain, massif, karst, badlands, sinkholes, a canyon) shows stone at any
   cliff and where it is very broken, scree where it is rough. A plateau rung in
   soft country changes nothing: the meadow runs up to the edge.
4. Dunes are **sand** where the warmth is at least the cold line; colder, the
   ridges stay and wear the climate's ground, a frozen dune field. Badlands,
   karst and sinkhole country are scree.
5. A delta's fan is the wet ground of its climate row.
6. **Tors**: on plains and hills, where a fine noise pattern peaks (about one
   cell in a hundred), stone: building stone where there is no rock landform.
7. Water in excess, in patches that a noise pattern decides cell by cell:
   **bog** on the cold-to-cool half of the warmth range (warmth under 140,
   moisture 190 or more), **marsh** on the warm-to-hot half (moisture 230 or
   more, within two cells of fresh water, on flat ground). There are more bogs
   than marshes by design.
8. The climate grid, warmth against moisture:

| warmth | dry (under 90) | balanced | wet (170 and over) | beside water |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| frigid (under 85) | tundra | tundra | tundra | |
| cold (under 115) | tundra | heath | moorland | |
| temperate | steppe | meadow | grass | |
| hot (185 and over) | dust | savanna | verdure (moisture 200 and over), savanna under it | floodplain within three cells of a river or lake |

Past the ends, hot ground at 220 or more is sand unless it is verdure or a
floodplain. A floodplain has its own warmth line (170) so the bank and the strip
behind it read the same, and any floodplain patch that does not touch fresh
water through other floodplain is wiped to savanna. On the dial, a warmth of 0
is nearly all tundra; heath and moorland are what 0.25 looks like.

**Names.** The Domain, each district and each body of water get placeholder
names from syllable lists, deterministic per seed, so the output can be talked
about; the culture layer will replace them.

### Stage 13: overhangs and arches

The only stage that gives a column a second run of solid ground, run last
because the lip of an overhang is a roof, not ground. A columnar model cannot
cut sideways into a cliff, so it is built the other way round: the columns in
front of a face of 8 slabs or more get a second run up at the cliff top, a lip
two slabs thick with four slabs of air under it. From below that is an undercut;
from above, the cliff edge jutting out. An **arch** joins two cliff tops within
two slabs of each other across a gorge or a channel, up to four cells, never
over open aether. Both need backing (the high side must have two neighbours near
its own top and must not be a landform whose whole shape is the wall, like a
karst tower), or a lip off a two-cell tower reads as a hole punched through it.
What this stage adds is not walkable; walking over a two-level column is its own
problem, not yet solved.

### The guarantees

After all that, five things are checked, and a failure triggers the re-roll:

| guarantee | why |
|---|---|
| exactly one Entry, of the kind and on the edge asked for | a Link whose ends disagree is not a Link |
| at least one Exit, as many as asked, of the kind asked | a Domain with no way onward is a dead end in a tree |
| a road from the Entry to every Exit | an Exit you cannot reach is the same dead end |
| a district on the heartland | somewhere the first company can be laid out |
| the heartland covers at least 75% of the dry land | below that there is a second island nobody asked for |

Nothing else is re-rolled for.

## 4. Why it is built this way (the appendix, briefly)

- **Elevation is not a smooth field that gets quantised.** Rounding a smooth
  height map to slabs gives cliffs wherever the noise happens to be steep. Here
  every patch is built under its own slope limit, so cliffs come only from the
  rung ladder and from sculpting, and mean something.
- **Ties in the river flood break on noise**, because a plain flood is a
  breadth-first search whose tree is a fan of straight rays.
- **Lowering, never raising.** Every correction pass only lowers ground, which
  is why the settle loops are guaranteed to stop.
- **The channel sinks with its valley**, or you get a moat.
- **A navigable river holds one level** across its two cells, or the draught is
  lost.
- **Water pours every way it plausibly can**, but never onto dry ground.
- **Lakes that are not one big lake**: the shape roll.
- **Goo never mixes**, three ways: placement, the river mask, the audit.
- **The cube has a lid**, and the Gates are the first thing it bites.
- **The fit band wraps the linker**, since the linker shrinks every scattered
  layout.
- **Beaches are ground, and the sculpted rock is scree**: sand beaches drew a
  yellow strand round tundra; dust on karst put hot ground beside snow.
- **The chills were the label**: an earlier warmth model cooled every cell by a
  permanent twenty points, so the dial lied; now the label is the open lowland.
- **The lapse per mountain**, from its own foot, after three earlier models put
  the snow line in the wrong place at some footprint or other.
- **The knobs roll**, so sixty audited islands are sixty climates, not sixty
  shapes of one.
- **Shelves gave way to districts**; **the lee is a rain shadow**; **a lake may
  swallow a river**; **a delta is cut, not found**; **sea stacks are aether**;
  **bog on the cold side, marsh on the warm**; **hot water on a cold Domain**;
  **cold sand and cold floodplain** fixed after the co-occurrence count caught
  them.

Things tried and removed include ramps (replaced by passes), lake chains, a
single "fragmentation" number (replaced by named arrangements), craters and a
volcanic character, streams fordable everywhere, a berth wherever the domino
fits, arches over open aether, a Spiral and a Broken Fractal layout, a
Domain-wide fluid setting, dropping the Gate edge band outright, geysers as
terrain, shelves, and the damper lee.

## 5. How a change is trusted

Two scenes run without a window, from a shell, and a third is the human end.

### The checksum

`generation_checksum.tscn` builds 446 islands across the whole settings matrix
(60 default seeds; every arrangement against every character at 64²; all three
sizes; every Gate request; every crossings setting; both ends of every dial and
of every whole-number setting; the older layouts and landforms with the newer
ones switched off) and hashes every field of every island. A change meant to
leave generation alone must report "0 of 446 islands moved". A change meant to
alter it records the new hashes with `-- accept` and says so in its commit. It
is the bit-for-bit gate. Determinism hangs on details a refactor can break
silently: hash salts, noise seed offsets, the order of floating-point
expressions, scan and neighbour order, an unstable sort, dictionary order. When
in doubt, run the checksum.

### The audit

`generation_audit.tscn` runs the real generator over 60 seeds at 128² with every
dial rolled, so the sixty sample the whole range, and prints the measured
guarantees as numbers rather than re-implementing anything: the step grammar
(what share of neighbouring cells are free steps, two-slab steps, cliffs, and
where the cliffs fall by landform pair); patch sizes; mesa clearance and basin
drop; hill relief; the sculpted landforms' wall heights; mountain rise and step
profile; rivers (cells, navigable cells, falls, water running uphill, courses
reaching the rim, straightness, eyots, lakes that swallow a river, deltas,
springs, fords per hundred stream cells on flat and broken ground); ferries;
overhangs; material shares and anchor counts (with tors and hot water); the
per-island means of every climate axis, plus what the sun, the wind and the
hollows did; lakes and goo (leaks, water touching the void, goo touching water,
all "want 0"); altitude against the box; gorges and sealed gorges; what each
character delivered; walkability and reach; passes; districts; crossings; every
Gate rule; roads; arrangements; re-rolls; continuity. Any "want 0" that is not
zero names its seed as it happens, so it can be built in the lab. It ends by
diffing thirty-odd headline numbers against `docs/audit-baseline.json`; that is
a diff, not a test, and `AcceptBaseline` records the current numbers as the new
reference.

### The sweeps

Optional runs, each a flag on the audit that can be given after `--` on the
command line:

- **Silhouettes**: an ASCII outline of one island per arrangement.
  **Waterways**: one island's water at full resolution, a character per cell.
- **Sculpts**: a digit height map of one patch of each sculpted landform.
- **Feasibility**: every arrangement against every character, a few seeds
  each: attempts, giving up, and how much is reachable.
- **Gate requests**, **Gate matrix**, the four-hanging test, the funnel and
  the reductions: every Entry edge and kind, every Exit count and kind,
  reported as delivered.
- **Knobs**: lakes, rivers, crossings, valleys and wind each stepped from 0 to
  1 with everything else held, so a dial whose column does not move is caught.
- **Climate**: material shares at the climate corners (cold, temperate, hot by
  dry, balanced, wet, plus the cool, warm and frigid wet corners and the two
  ends).
- **Bulk**, **Sizes**, **Debut**, **Strain**: land per arrangement; the
  guarantee set at every footprint (with the share of snow at each, since the
  snow line has to exist at 64² as well as 128²); the newest shapes at every
  footprint and character; where re-rolls cluster.
- **Portraits** and **Field maps**: top-view pictures of islands, and the
  climate axes, anchors and surfaces as pictures.
- **Gallery** (and **masks**): a contact sheet of sixteen seeds per
  arrangement, captioned with the landmass count, and beside it the raw outlines
  before anything downstream touched them, which is how a shape that comes out
  wrong gets blamed on the layout or on what came after.
- **Climate grid** and **Climate scout**: one seed generated twenty-five times
  at every pair of moisture and warmth in quarters, with a strip of the seven
  fields the surface is read from; the scout scores seeds for variety so the
  seed is chosen, not guessed.
- **Climate chart**: the grid itself as an area, drawn straight from the rule
  the surface stage uses.
- **Climate stats**: the mean material shares at the twenty-five dial
  positions over thirty seeds each, and the co-occurrence matrix over five
  hundred rolled seeds (given one ground, how often each other is present).
- **Arrangement sheet**, **Knob sheet**, **Stage sheet**: every layout for one
  seed; every dial swept for one seed; one island drawn after each stage of the
  pipeline through a read-only hook in the generator.

Labels on these pictures are drawn by a tiny bitmap font, since headless Godot
has no rendering device.

### The lab

`island_lab.tscn` (F6 in the editor) is the human end: an island drawn as one
box per slab-run with a control panel that writes the same settings the audit
measures. Fourteen views (height, landform, region, walk, reach, surface,
anchors, and the seven fields: moisture, warmth, ruggedness, exposure, rim,
water distance, magick), each with a legend in its actual colours; overlays for
bridge sites, landings, ferries, roads, fords, and the compass with the wind,
the sun, the dune grain and the two boxes; a liquid toggle that shows the beds;
a seed field; and a readout that says what the island turned out to be, down to
what each road out costs. Every dial has an Auto box, and after a build the
slider sits at what the seed rolled.

## 6. What the numbers say now

Measured on the last accepted audit (60 seeds, 128², all dials rolled):

- **Steps**: 92.7% of neighbouring pairs are free steps, 1.1% two-slab (all at
  riverbanks or beside landforms whose height is the point), 6.3% cliffs.
- **Rivers** on every island; every island's rivers reach the rim; 0 channels
  uncut and 0 water running uphill; 8 lakes swallow a river on 8 islands; 17
  deltas on 12 islands; 218 springs; fords at 11 per hundred stream cells on
  flat ground and 6 on broken.
- **Ground**: grass 21.5%, stone 11.2%, meadow 9.8%, dust 8.4%, tundra 8.2%,
  scree 5.7%, sand 5.3%, silt 4.7%, heath 4.5%, moorland 4.4%, steppe 4.4%,
  savanna 4.3%, verdure 2.4%, bog 2.1%, floodplain 2.0%, snow 0.8%, marsh 0.4%.
- **Walkability**: 42.6% of land on the mainland on foot; 94.8% on the
  heartland once built; 47 of 60 islands one reachable whole; 455 districts on
  the heartland, every island with at least one.
- **Gates**: every island one Entry and three Exits, none on a shared edge, off
  the heartland, outside the box or short of a level strip; a road to every
  Exit, 68 of 180 walkable with nothing built.
- **Climate**: sunny slopes 8 warmer than shaded; from still air to a gale the
  flat lee dries by 32 while open ground holds; 109 cells of hot water on 11
  of the 20 cold islands.
- **Re-rolls**: every seed built in one attempt, none unplayable.

Known gaps: a few two-slab steps where the ground the fix would cut is a
landform, a bridgehead or standing water; one islet adrift on the most broken
layout; five sealed gorge reaches whose rims are misaligned; deltas small and
rare; sea stacks rare because the crop seldom leaves a speck; overhangs not
walkable by design.

## 7. Glossary

- **Domain**: one floating island, the thing generated. **Ecumene**: the whole
  tree of them. **Aether**: the void between, hazardous to people.
- **Link** and **Gate**: the route between two Domains and the built structure
  at each end; hanging Gates are flown through, land Gates walked through.
- **Cell**, **column**, **slab**, **run**: the grid square, the stack of ground
  on it, the quarter-cell block it is counted in, and one solid stretch of it.
- **Free step**: one slab, the step that costs nothing. **Cliff**: two or more.
- **Rung** and **ladder**: the terrace levels patches stand on.
- **Patch** or **region**: one county of the island's patchwork, with one
  landform.
- **Effective level**: the water surface where there is water, the ground
  elsewhere.
- **Walk area**, **district**, **reach area**, **mainland**, **heartland**: what
  connects on foot; a walk area big enough to build on; what connects once
  built; the walk area under the Entry; the reach area under the Entry.
- **Works**: stairs, bridges, ferries. **Apron**: the district a Gate's landing
  opens onto.
- **Anchor**: a list of cells of one kind the content layer will read.
- **Knob** or **dial**: a 0-to-1 setting; **Auto** lets the seed roll it.
- **Seed**: the number that, with the settings, determines the whole island.
- **Checksum**, **audit**, **baseline**: the bit-for-bit gate, the measured
  guarantees, and the last accepted numbers they are diffed against.
